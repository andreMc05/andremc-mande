**The future of software is less “buy a package and mold it” and more “describe what you need and have AI generate it.”**

For years the default was: purchase a SaaS suite that covers most of your requirements, then spend significant time and money customizing, integrating, and working around the gaps while waiting on the vendor for missing features. Feature requests sat in queues. Pricing scaled with seats. Exact fit was rare.

That model is under measurable pressure.

### Evidence of the shift

- **McKinsey’s 2026 State of AI survey** (1,719 respondents): 32% of organizations have already decided against buying at least one software product or feature because they could build the capability internally with agentic coding tools. The figure is 41% in technology and 39% in healthcare.

- **Retool’s 2026 Build vs. Buy report** (817 builders across roles and company sizes): 35% of teams have already replaced at least one SaaS tool with a custom build, and 78% expect to build more custom internal tools in 2026. Leading categories include workflow automation, internal admin tools, and BI/reporting dashboards. Roughly half report saving 6+ hours per week via AI-assisted building; 60% had created tools outside traditional IT oversight.

- **Gartner**: Up to $234 billion in enterprise application SaaS spending is exposed to “agentic arbitrage” between now and 2030 (roughly 20% of the category by the end of the decade). Agents can complete cross-system work and deliver outcomes, reducing reliance on traditional UX-heavy interfaces and seat-based models. Gartner frames this as a metamorphosis rather than pure destruction of SaaS.

- **Coding capability is already high**: JetBrains’ 2026 Developer Ecosystem Survey (~15,000 professional developers) found that, on average, ~47% of code is fully written by AI agents. Google has stated that 75% of its new code is AI-generated and approved by engineers. Stack Overflow’s 2026 survey showed 73% of respondents who use AI coding tools/agents do so daily.

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella recently described an “infinite SaaS factory”: describe what the business needs in Copilot (including its Code experience) and generate a customization or entirely new module connected to existing systems of record. The framing is a move toward more “personal” software that fits how teams actually work, while staying linked to core data and logic layers rather than fragmenting them.

Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark has argued that AI pushes software away from one-size-fits-all applications toward tools generated on demand for a specific person, team, task, or workflow—users describe the need and receive software shaped to their exact context.

### Practical implications

Describe the precise need in natural language → working prototype in hours or days instead of months. Iterate and add features as requirements change without external vendor timelines or renegotiation. For many internal tools, operational workflows, dashboards, and process-specific applications, the speed and total cost of ownership calculus has shifted.

This does **not** mean every SaaS product disappears. Core systems of record with strong regulatory, security, network-effect, or infrastructure moats (certain ERP functions, payments, identity, etc.) remain difficult and often unwise to replace. Governance, security, data quality, long-term maintenance, and the risk of unmanaged “shadow” tools remain real constraints. Not every build succeeds, and purchased solutions still have higher success rates in some analyses.

The organizations that navigate this well will treat AI as a way to make software fit the work (and evolve with it), rather than forcing work into a packaged mold—and they will retain more direct control over that evolution.

What parts of your current stack are still being forced into a package that was never designed for your exact process?
